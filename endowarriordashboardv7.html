💡 Tips for Finding the Right Specialist
  • For endometriosis-related infertility, look for an REI who also collaborates with an excision surgeon — the combination produces the best outcomes.
  • For male factor infertility, seek a urologist with fellowship training in male reproductive medicine, not just a general urologist.
  • ASRM member status and ABOG board certification in REI are the two most important credentials to verify.
  • Ask clinics for their live birth rate per transfer — not just their pregnancy rate — when comparing IVF programs.

💡 Tips for Finding an IC Specialist
  • Look for a urologist or urogynecologist with specific IC experience — not all urology practices treat IC well.
  • IC and endometriosis frequently co-exist. Ideally your IC specialist and your excision surgeon communicate with each other.
  • A good IC specialist will offer a multimodal treatment plan — diet, physical therapy, medications, and possibly instillations or neuromodulation.
  • The ICA's patient community is often the best source for local specialist recommendations — ask in their forums.
  • IC has no cure but is very manageable with the right specialist and treatment combination.

💡 Tips for Finding a Vulvodynia Specialist
  • Vulvodynia is best treated by a multidisciplinary team — typically a gynecologist, pelvic floor PT, and sometimes a pain psychologist working together.
  • Look for providers who specifically list vulvodynia, vestibulodynia, or chronic vulvar pain as a specialty — general GYNs often miss it.
  • The NVA directory is the single best starting point — it only lists providers who have specifically sought out vulvodynia training.
  • If you are also dealing with endometriosis, ask your excision surgeon for a referral — endo and vulvodynia frequently co-occur and an integrated approach works best.
  • Don't give up if the first specialist doesn't help — vulvodynia treatment is highly individual and finding the right combination takes persistence.

💡 What to Look for in a Pelvic Floor PT
  • Look for a PT with specialized pelvic floor training — not all physical therapists treat pelvic floor conditions.
  • Herman & Wallace certification (PRPC) or APTA Women's Health board certification are the strongest credentials.
  • Ask if they have experience with endometriosis, IC, or vulvodynia specifically — not just general pelvic floor work.

  • Many pelvic PTs now offer telehealth for education and exercises, with in-person visits for hands-on treatment.

Understanding the difference between these two surgical approaches is one of the most important things an endo patient can know. They are not the same — and the difference has a profound impact on outcomes.

What is Excision?

Excision (also called laparoscopic excision or LAPEX) involves a surgeon carefully cutting out endometriosis lesions in their entirety — roots and all — using scissors, a scalpel, or a laser as a precision cutting tool. Because the entire lesion is removed, there is no remaining tissue to regrow from. This is considered the gold-standard surgical treatment for endometriosis by leading specialists worldwide. It requires advanced training, significant surgical skill, and a deep understanding of pelvic anatomy.

🔥

What is Ablation?

Ablation (also called fulguration or cauterization) uses heat, electrical energy, or laser to burn the surface of visible endometriosis lesions. It does not remove the underlying disease — only destroys what is visible on top. Because endometriosis lesions grow in three dimensions and often extend deeper than what the eye can see, ablation typically leaves disease behind. Most patients experience pain relief for months to a few years before symptoms return. Ablation is far more widely performed than excision simply because it requires less training — not because it produces better outcomes.

💡 Why Does This Matter to You?

Many women with endometriosis undergo multiple ablation surgeries over years or decades — each one providing temporary relief, each one potentially causing additional scar tissue, and none of them addressing the root cause. This is sometimes called the "ablation cycle."

When you see a general OB/GYN for endometriosis surgery, ablation is often the default — not because it's the best option, but because it's the most common skill taught in residency. True excision surgeons are a smaller, specialized group who have sought out advanced training specifically for this purpose.

The most important question to ask any surgeon before consenting to endometriosis surgery: "Will you be removing the lesions completely by excision, or burning/destroying them by ablation?" If they cannot clearly answer "excision," keep looking.

📋 What the Research Shows

Multiple peer-reviewed studies have demonstrated significantly lower recurrence rates and better long-term pain outcomes with excision compared to ablation. A landmark study by Dr. Andrew Cook and colleagues found that patients treated with excision experienced dramatically better outcomes than those treated with ablation alone. The AAGL, ASRM, and leading endo specialists worldwide now recognize excision as the preferred surgical approach for endometriosis.

Hysterectomy & Endometriosis

One of the most persistent and harmful myths in endometriosis care is that a hysterectomy is a cure. It is not. Understanding why is critical before consenting to any major surgery.

❌

The Myth: "A Hysterectomy Will Cure Your Endometriosis"

This statement is told to thousands of women with endometriosis every year by well-meaning physicians who do not specialize in the disease. It is factually incorrect and leads to irreversible surgery that fails to resolve symptoms in a significant number of patients.

✔️

The Reality: Endometriosis Lives Outside the Uterus

Endometriosis is not a uterine disease. It is a systemic condition where endometriosis-like tissue implants on the ovaries, fallopian tubes, bowel, bladder, peritoneum, diaphragm, and other structures throughout the pelvis and body. Removing the uterus does nothing to address the disease that lives outside it. If endometriosis lesions are not excised at the time of hysterectomy, the disease — and the pain — remains.

📋 What the Research Shows

Studies show that up to 62% of women who have a hysterectomy for endometriosis continue to experience pain when the lesions outside the uterus are not removed. Many women report no improvement in their primary symptoms. A hysterectomy without concurrent thorough excision of all visible disease is widely considered inadequate treatment by leading endometriosis specialists.

⚡ What About Ovary Removal?

Removing the ovaries (oophorectomy) to eliminate estrogen is sometimes recommended on the theory that endo is "estrogen-dependent." However, the body continues producing estrogen through other pathways even after ovary removal, and surgical menopause carries serious long-term risks including cardiovascular disease, bone density loss, and cognitive effects. Most excision specialists strongly advise against prophylactic oophorectomy in premenopausal women.

💕 When Hysterectomy May Be Appropriate

There are situations where a hysterectomy may be genuinely appropriate — for example, if adenomyosis (endometriosis within the uterine muscle) is the primary source of pain, or if there are other uterine conditions like fibroids causing significant symptoms. Even in these cases, the best outcomes occur when thorough excision of all endometriosis lesions is performed at the same time by a specialist.

👪 Fertility Considerations

A hysterectomy is irreversible and permanently ends the ability to carry a pregnancy. For women who may want children in the future, this makes excision surgery — which can be performed while preserving the uterus and ovaries — the overwhelmingly preferred approach. Many women successfully conceive after expert excision surgery. A hysterectomy should never be recommended as a first-line treatment when excision has not been properly attempted.

🌿 What to Do Instead

  • Seek evaluation by a fellowship-trained excision surgeon — not a general OB/GYN — before consenting to any major surgery
  • Ask specifically: "Is it possible my pain can be addressed with excision while preserving my uterus?"
  • If adenomyosis is suspected, ask for an MRI to assess the extent of uterine involvement before making any surgical decision
  • Get a second opinion from a specialist on the iCareBetter, EndofEndo, or Nancy's Nook directories
  • Know that most leading excision surgeons will try conservative surgery first — and recommend hysterectomy only when truly necessary and all other options have been exhausted

This page is for educational purposes only and is not medical advice. There are individual circumstances where a hysterectomy is the right decision, and that is between you and a qualified specialist. The goal of this page is to ensure you have accurate information before making an irreversible choice — and that you have access to a specialist who can help you weigh all options.
